Why Your RAG Keeps Lying to You (And How to Make It Stop)

July 29, 2026 10 min read

A few weeks ago a researcher I know hit a wall. He'd built a small question-answering system on his own GPU, wired up an open-source Llama model, pointed it at his documents, and asked it questions. It answered confidently. It also answered wrong — inventing facts that were nowhere in his data.

This is one of the most common failures in applied AI, and the frustrating part is that the model isn't broken. It's doing exactly what it was designed to do. To understand why it lies, you first have to understand what it's actually doing — and once you do, the fix stops being guesswork.

Let me walk you through it the way I'd explain it to a smart friend over coffee.

First, what is RAG, really?

A large language model like Llama or GPT is, at heart, a very sophisticated pattern-completer. It learned from a fixed snapshot of text and it carries that knowledge frozen in its weights. Ask it something it never saw, or something specific to your documents, and it doesn't say "I don't know." It does the one thing it always does: it produces the most plausible-sounding continuation. Plausible is not the same as true. That gap is what we call a hallucination.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is the fix. The idea is simple and elegant: instead of trusting the model's memory, you give it an open-book exam. Before the model answers, you retrieve the relevant pages from your own documents and hand them to it along with the question. Now the model isn't recalling — it's reading. RAG augments the language model with fresh, trusted data pulled from an external knowledge source, so the answer is grounded in something real instead of invented.

Every RAG system, from a weekend prototype to a system serving millions, is built from the same handful of parts.

The architecture, in plain English

Think of RAG in two phases: preparing the library, and answering the question.

Phase 1 — Building the library (this happens once, ahead of time):

  1. Ingest and chunk. You take your raw documents — PDFs, notes, manuals — and slice them into smaller pieces called chunks. A chunk might be a paragraph or a section. Why slice? Because you'll be feeding these pieces to the model later, and it can only hold so much text at once.
  2. Embed. Each chunk is run through an embedding model (an encoder) that turns the text into a list of numbers — a vector. The clever bit: chunks that mean similar things end up as vectors that sit close together in mathematical space. "The dog barked" and "the canine howled" land near each other even though they share no words.
  3. Store. You save all these vectors in a vector database so you can search them fast.

Phase 2 — Answering a question (this happens every time someone asks something):

  1. Retrieve. The user's question gets embedded into a vector too. The system finds the chunks whose vectors are closest to the question's vector — usually measured by cosine similarity, which is just a way of asking "which of these point in the same direction as my question?" You grab the top few. That "top few" is a knob called top-k (top-3, top-5, and so on).
  2. Augment. You paste those retrieved chunks into the prompt, alongside the original question.
  3. Generate. The language model reads the question and the supporting material, and writes an answer grounded in what you gave it.

Query goes in → relevant documents come out of the library → question and documents go to the model together → grounded answer comes out. That's the whole game.

If you want to see this drawn out cleanly, these are the clearest architecture references I've found:

So why was the researcher's system still hallucinating?

Here's the key insight most beginners miss: if the model gets the wrong pages, it will confidently answer from the wrong pages. RAG doesn't stop hallucination by magic — it stops it only if retrieval succeeds. Bad retrieval in, bad answer out. So when a RAG system lies, the problem is usually in the plumbing before the model, not the model itself.

Let's diagnose it in the order I'd actually check things.

1. Chunk size — the most underrated knob

If your chunks are too big, each one contains several ideas, and its embedding becomes a muddy average of all of them — good at matching nothing in particular. If chunks are too small, you slice a single fact in half and neither piece makes sense on its own. There's no universal right answer; it depends entirely on your documents. Dense technical text often wants smaller chunks; flowing narrative wants larger ones. Tuning this was good advice.

2. Top-k — how many pages you hand over

Retrieve too few chunks and you might miss the one that held the answer. Retrieve too many and you bury the useful chunk under noise — and models genuinely get distracted by irrelevant context. Nudging top-k up or down is a legitimate lever. Also good advice.

3. The retriever itself — dense vs. sparse, and BM25

This is where it gets interesting. Everything above uses dense retrieval — matching by meaning via embeddings. There's an older, complementary approach called sparse retrieval, and its most famous workhorse is BM25. BM25 matches by keywords — it's essentially a very refined version of counting how often and how distinctively your query words appear in a document.

Why does this matter? Dense retrieval is great at meaning but can whiff on exact terms — product codes, drug names, error numbers, rare acronyms. BM25 nails those because it's literally looking for the word. The strongest real-world systems use hybrid retrieval: run both, then combine the results. If the researcher's documents are full of precise terminology, adding BM25 alongside the embeddings could easily be the single biggest fix. Your workshop pointed you at something real.

4. The encoder — the quiet game-changer

The embedding model that turns text into vectors is called the encoder, and which one you pick matters enormously. A general-purpose encoder trained on web text has a fuzzy sense of specialized vocabulary. Feed it biomedical documents and it can't tell two different proteins apart, because it never learned they were different — so it retrieves the wrong chunk, and then the model hallucinates from it.

This is why domain-specific encoders exist. SapBERT and BioLORD, for instance, are tuned on biomedical text and understand that vocabulary the way a specialist would. Your friend was right: for a specialized corpus, swapping a generic encoder for a domain one can transform retrieval quality. The rule of thumb — it all depends on the type of dataset — is exactly the instinct a senior engineer develops. There is no best encoder; there's only the best encoder for your data.

5. Your storage shortcut — is it fine?

Your friend mentioned that for small datasets he just pickles a dictionary and runs cosine similarity directly, skipping a fancy vector database. This is completely legitimate. A dedicated vector database earns its keep at scale — millions of chunks, many users, constant updates. For a few thousand chunks on one researcher's GPU, a plain in-memory search is simpler, faster to set up, and has fewer moving parts to break. Knowing when you don't need the heavy machinery is itself a mark of experience. Don't let anyone shame you into infrastructure you don't need yet.

What about that "copy-cat model" and "garbage prompt" business?

This part of the conversation drifted into a related but separate topic: distillation. It's worth clearing up because it comes up in interviews and it's easy to garble.

Distillation is how you take a big, expensive "teacher" model and train a small, cheap "student" model to imitate it. You feed the same prompts to the teacher, capture its responses, and train the student to reproduce them. The student ends up punching well above its size — it inherits the teacher's behavior without the teacher's cost. That's why people call distilled models "copy-cats": the small model is, in a real sense, copying a bigger one.

The "garbage prompt" phrasing your friend used is a fuzzy pointer at this: you can probe a model with a huge variety of prompts — including weird or synthetic ones — to extract its behavior and train a student on the results. The claim that some Chinese labs distill from other models is a widely discussed and contested talking point, not settled fact, so I'd treat it as industry gossip rather than something to assert in an article. The technique of distillation, though, is completely mainstream and worth knowing.

Two things to keep straight, because interviewers love the distinction:

  • Distillation shrinks a model. It has nothing directly to do with hallucination.
  • RAG grounds a model. It's about what the model reads, not how big it is.

Mixing these up is a classic beginner tell. Keeping them separate is a small signal that you actually understand the field.

Now — was the advice to the researcher right?

Let's grade it honestly, because this is the part that turns a reader into a decision-maker.

"Tune chunk size and top-k" — right, and the correct first move. Cheap, fast, no new components. Always start here.

"Consider BM25 / hybrid retrieval" — right, and often the highest-impact fix when documents contain precise terminology. This is where I'd go next.

"Change the encoder to a domain-specific one" — right, especially for specialized data. If retrieval is pulling the wrong chunks, a better encoder fixes the disease; everything downstream is treating symptoms.

"Switch from Llama to at least GPT-4o-mini" — this one deserves a caveat. It might help, but it's the least targeted move on the list, and here's the reasoning a senior engineer would give: if the retrieval is handing over the wrong chunks, a smarter generator will just hallucinate more eloquently from the wrong material. A better model does not fix bad retrieval. So while GPT-4o-mini is a strong, cheap, well-behaved model and a perfectly reasonable choice, swapping the generator should be last, not first — after you've confirmed retrieval is actually feeding it the right pages. Otherwise you're paying an API bill to make the same mistake with better grammar.

There's also a self-hosting angle worth naming: the researcher chose Llama on his own GPU for a reason — privacy, cost, control. Moving to a hosted API means his data leaves the building. That's not a dealbreaker, but it's a real trade-off, not a free upgrade.

The decision framework to take away

If you remember nothing else, remember the order of operations. When a RAG system hallucinates, fix it from the retrieval side inward, because that's where the problem almost always lives and where the cheapest fixes are:

  1. Look at what got retrieved first. Before touching anything, inspect the chunks the system actually pulled for a failing question. Were they relevant? This one step tells you whether your problem is retrieval or generation, and saves you from fixing the wrong half.
  2. Tune chunking and top-k. Cheap, immediate, no new parts.
  3. Add hybrid retrieval (dense + BM25). Especially if your data has precise terms.
  4. Upgrade the encoder to match your domain. The fix with the deepest leverage on specialized data.
  5. Only then, consider a stronger generator. Once you know the model is reading the right material and still fumbling.

The mental model underneath all of it: a language model can only be as truthful as the context you feed it. RAG hallucinations are, nine times out of ten, retrieval failures wearing a generation costume. Fix what the model reads, and the lying mostly takes care of itself.

The researcher's system wasn't broken. It was just reading the wrong pages — and now you know exactly how to hand it the right ones.
